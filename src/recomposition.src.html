The Recomposition

A mothership climbs back out of a black hole and reassembles in real time, around a black hole ray-traced from Einstein’s equations.

Real physics

  • The black hole: every pixel’s light is traced through the Schwarzschild solution of Einstein’s equations, with Doppler shift, redshift and beaming on the disk. It’s the same engine as Event Horizon.
  • Faithful version: about 2,000 points across the ship each fall freely on their own orbit around this black hole, computed from Einstein’s equations. Nothing is scripted: the stretching, squeezing and winding are what tides do. The story plays that fall backward.
  • Each piece appears where its light actually reaches you, using exact light paths. Pieces behind the hole are hidden by the shadow and show up as bent images around its edge, plus a ghost image on the far side. The glowing disk hides whatever is behind it.
  • The pieces aren’t hot. They’re lit by the disk, and each piece’s light is shifted by gravity and by its own speed, both taken from the simulation. Falling debris fades exponentially as it nears the horizon, which is why the story opens dark.
  • The ship’s path is aimed so all of it is swallowed. On a path that grazes the capture threshold, tides would fling about half the ship back out to space; that is real too, but played backward it looks like debris raining in from nowhere.
  • Playback speed varies like a time-lapse: the long crawl near the horizon plays fast, and the story slows to the real pace as the ship forms.

Imagined

  • Nothing comes out of a black hole. Its time-reverse is a white hole, which the equations allow but no one has ever observed.
  • The ship, its welding and the engines that stop it are invented, and so is running the fall backward. The pieces don’t collide with each other or with the disk’s gas, and positions are shown on a distant observer’s clock without the extra delay for light travel time.
  • The stylized version sends threads from all over the horizon along invented paths, and doesn’t bend their light.
  • Near a black hole the mass of the Sun, tides would shred a ship well before the horizon. Only giant black holes are gentle enough to cross intact.
